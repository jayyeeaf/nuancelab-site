Working list · October 2026

Affordances for moving together

Small, practical things that help everyday people feel at ease moving to music in parks, sports parks and other public spaces, and that help a whole floor find one groove.

A nuance.lab resource · part of the thinking behind the Dance Commons

What we mean by affordances

An affordance is what a place offers you: what it makes easy, or quietly invites you to do. (The word comes from the psychologist James J. Gibson.) A low wall affords sitting. A smooth floor and a steady beat afford dancing. A bench with a good view of the floor affords watching first and joining later.

Most adults who say “I don’t dance” are not short of ability; the capacity is latent, and grows with scaffolding. What often holds people back is the feeling of being watched and judged, and the lack of ordinary places where moving to music is simply what happens. The affordances below are small design and hosting choices that lower that threshold, so people can move expressively and exploratorily, at their own pace, among others.

From design research on urban sports

Seen, but not watched

“Schaduw, zichtlijnen, zitranden en flow bepalen of iemand twee minuten blijft staan of twee uur blijft hangen. Een plek klopt als je je gezien voelt maar niet bekeken, als verblijven vanzelfsprekend is en beweging uitgenodigd wordt.”

“Shade, sightlines, seating edges and flow determine whether someone stops for two minutes or hangs around for two hours. A place works when you feel seen but not watched, when lingering comes naturally and movement is invited.”

That is the feeling we design for. It is easy to agree with and hard to build, which is why this list leans on hosts as much as on hardware, and why we keep trying, observing and adjusting.

Daniel Casas-Valle (Urban Dynamics) & Guido Schuurman (Atelier OUI), Urban Sport als ontmoeting! (2026), p. 3. Translation ours.

Where to start

If you can only do a few things, start with people, then add a few basics. The strongest combination we have found is a weekly session with a named host, at the same time and place, plus a handful of physical affordances:

  • somewhere to sit and watch at the edge;
  • a smooth patch of floor;
  • a shared beat at a sensible volume;
  • some shelter and warm light.

Most of that can arrive on a cargo bike and leave again afterwards. Why hosts first? Equipment left alone in public space tends to get damaged or taken over, while a place that is used and looked after every week tends to look after itself. In the Dance Commons we call this being stewarded by its hosts.

Group 1

Space and surfaces

How the floor and its edges are shaped decides whether people feel invited or on display.

  • Watch-then-join seatingResearch

    Low walls, steps, benches or movable crates at the edge of the floor, facing the action.

    Why it helps. Newcomers can watch without being “on”, get a feel for the music and the people, and join when they are ready. People sitting nearby also keep a friendly eye on the place. Several small clusters work better than one grandstand, which can turn into a judging gallery.

    Sources: William H. Whyte’s observations of city plazas, Sitwalls, Ledges & Steps (Project for Public Spaces); Casas-Valle & Schuurman (2026), including the Streetmekka example where parents sat on concrete blocks to watch.

  • A smooth patch of floorPrecedent

    A defined smooth, non-slip surface: interlocking outdoor dance tiles or a roll-out floor to begin with, perhaps a fixed smooth pad later.

    Why it helps. It says “dancing happens here” without a sign, is kinder to joints and shoes, and makes the space readable for people who do not skate. Portable tiles that hosts lay and lift each session also avoid the floor being skated over.

    Sources: Urban Sports Park Roosendaal includes a streetdance floor (Nine Yards); Leuven uses pop-up breaking floors (ESWAM eMagazine).

  • Several small zones, not one stageResearch

    Soft transitions between path, seating and floor, and two or three smaller floors rather than one central spotlight.

    Why it helps. People tend to overestimate how much others notice them. Small zones let someone start in a corner, and keep any one group from owning the space. Keeping every zone within earshot of the same sound lets the corners merge as confidence grows.

    Sources: Gilovich, Medvec & Savitsky (2000) on the spotlight effect; Casas-Valle & Schuurman (2026) on soft transition zones and several activities side by side; Make Space for Girls park guide.

  • A cypher at the edge, an open floor in the middleIdea to test

    A marked circle for cyphers and battles to one side, while the main floor stays open for everyone moving at once.

    Why it helps. It honours the cypher and its lineage without making it the only way in, so newcomers are not pushed into the centre and the whole floor can still build together. Turn-taking for the sound is agreed with local breakers.

    Our proposal. We have not found a direct test of this layout.

  • Temporary floor markingsIdea to test

    Washable chalk or tape laid by hosts: a circle, a few “start here” footprints, a grid for mirroring a partner.

    Why it helps. A wordless invitation and a simple script to begin with, which can turn “dancing” into a game. Markings that look too much like a playground could feel patronising to adults, so we would design them with local dancers.

    Related evidence: multicolour playground markings increased children’s activity at break time (Stratton & Mullan, 2005). Whether this carries over to adults moving to music is untested.

  • Mirror panels, optionalPrecedent

    Portable mirror panels at one edge of the floor, facing away from the seating, out only during sessions.

    Why it helps. Mirrors help people learn by copying the host and seeing themselves. They can also make people more self-conscious, so they stay at the edge and easy to avoid.

    Sources: mirror walls for street dance practice at a Kaohsiung station; dancers lost a rare free practice spot when mirrors at Singapore’s Bayfront MRT were frosted (CNA); Dancers’ Alley in Sydney.

Already built in the Netherlands

A streetdance floor in a sports park

Since 2021, the Urban Sports Park at Stadsoevers in Roosendaal, designed and built by Nine Yards for the municipality, has had a streetdance floor alongside a skatepark, a freerunning course and 3x3 basketball courts. It shows that dance can be planned into a sports park from the start, not squeezed in afterwards.

Nine Yards, Urban sports park Roosendaal.

Group 2

Sound and rhythm

A shared pulse is what turns a crowd of individuals into people moving together.

  • A shared beat at a sensible volumeResearch

    A battery speaker brought by the host, with a volume limiter, a set time window and a shared playlist. Headphones, silent-disco style, where neighbours are close.

    Why it helps. A shared beat lets everyone move to the same pulse. In a silent-disco experiment, people who danced in sync reported feeling closer to each other than people who danced out of sync. One host-held speaker also avoids competing sound systems, and short windows with the speaker pointed away from homes keep the neighbours on side.

    Sources: Tarr, Launay & Dunbar (2016), “Silent disco”, Evolution and Human Behavior; Hove & Risen (2009), “It’s all in the timing”, Social Cognition; the regular open-air milongas on the Quais de Seine, Paris.

  • Play that makes moving together musicalPrecedent

    A touring or seasonal installation where moving in time with others makes music, such as Montréal’s musical swings.

    Why it helps. It makes moving in sync playful without calling it dancing, a gentle way in for shy people. It is costly, so best as an occasional launch moment rather than the core.

    Source: Daily tous les jours, 21 Swings. Whether it leads to more dancing later is untested.

Group 3

Hosting and shared habits

People make a place feel open. These affordances are mostly habits, not hardware.

  • A weekly hosted sessionPrecedent

    Same time, same place, every week, opened and closed by named hosts, with a short optional warm-up for beginners: copy the host, find the beat.

    Why it helps. A fixed slot means you do not have to decide each time whether to go, and coming back is how regulars, and later new hosts, emerge. A beginner moment gives everyone permission to be bad at it for a while. Hosts are paid, work in pairs or a rota, and train the next ones, so it never rests on one person.

    Sources: Lindy in the Park, Golden Gate Park, San Francisco: free every Sunday since 1996, with a beginner lesson and no partner needed; Leuven’s free urban sports programme, including weekly sessions (ESWAM eMagazine); the Quais de Seine milongas.

  • Dance first, film laterPrecedent

    A friendly sign and a host habit: do not film others without asking; maybe a corner set aside for filming.

    Why it helps. Fear of being filmed keeps many people off the floor. A clear, shared norm eases that and takes some of the pressure to perform away. In public space it cannot be enforced, so it is an invitation that hosts model.

    Source: Berlin clubs put stickers over phone cameras to protect the dance floor (New York Times, 2024). Outdoors, this is still an idea.

  • A first hour for girls and mixed abilitiesResearch

    Recurring, announced slots hosted by women or by mixed-ability hosts.

    Why it helps. Girls and young women are strongly under-represented at most urban sports spaces, less from lack of interest than from the social dynamics on site. The feeling “this isn’t for me” sets in fast. A clear invitation helps, framed as a first hour rather than a closed door, and designed with the people it is for.

    Sources: Casas-Valle & Schuurman (2026); an Eindhoven urban sports park evaluation found more men than women visiting; SSNB’s Ruimte voor Meiden programme in Brabant.

  • A visible pulsePrecedent

    A small sign or QR code on site with the session time, the host’s name and a “come and watch first” line, linked to a simple calendar.

    Why it helps. It makes the session easy to find and easy to trust, which helps a newcomer decide to come. Hosts keep it up to date, with a clear way to post cancellations, because out-of-date information quickly erodes trust.

    Source: Leuven coordinates its urban sports calendar through an app (ESWAM eMagazine).

Group 4

Shelter, light and the basics

Small practical things often decide whether someone stays for two minutes or two hours.

  • Shade and rain coverPrecedent

    A pop-up canopy or sail from the kit, or a fixed, open-sided shelter next to the floor.

    Why it helps. Dutch weather is a real barrier. Somewhere to shelter makes it natural to stay longer, and staying is how visitors become regulars.

    Sources: Nine Yards’ urban sports park in Goes, with a sedum-roofed shelter and extra lighting for evening use; the Eindhoven evaluation noted a need for “een plek om te schuilen” (somewhere to shelter).

  • Warm, layered lightGuidance

    Ambient festoon or battery lights from the kit, or low-glare fixed lights on a timer. Not stage lighting.

    Why it helps. It makes evening sessions possible and helps the place feel safer, especially for women and girls, without putting anyone in a spotlight. Timers and downward shielding keep it neighbourly.

    Sources: Safer Parks guidance (via Newark & Sherwood District Council); Make Space for Girls park guide.

  • A toilet nearbyPrecedent

    An agreement with a nearby café or venue to open its toilets during sessions.

    Why it helps. A small, practical thing that often decides whether older people, women and families can stay for long.

    Source: UK community toilet schemes, in which councils pay local businesses to open their toilets to the public (Bristol City Council).

  • A place to meet the hostResearch

    A coffee bike or small kiosk where you can get a drink, borrow gear and find the host. No purchase needed to join.

    Why it helps. A reason to linger and a visible point of contact help passers-by become regulars.

    Sources: the Eindhoven evaluation found a need for a meeting place, such as a small café point; at Superkilen in Copenhagen, a mini-ramp sits right next to a café (Casas-Valle & Schuurman, 2026).

Group 5

Care behind the scenes

The quiet arrangements that let hosts keep going, week after week.

  • A kit on wheelsPrecedent

    A cargo bike, trailer or lockable container with the speaker, floor tiles, chalk, lights, first aid, bins and gear to borrow.

    Why it helps. The affordances arrive with the host and leave with them, so nothing is left out to be damaged, and being able to borrow gear lowers the threshold.

    Sources: Dutch municipal mobile sport containers, for example in Wijchen and Amsterdam; movable “Plug & Play” urban sports units (Casas-Valle & Schuurman, 2026). A dance version is still an idea.

  • Lockable storage on sitePrecedent

    A small locker, container or space in a partner building for the tiles, speaker and canopy.

    Why it helps. It makes a mobile kit practical without hauling it across town each week, while nothing stays exposed when no host is there.

    Source: Casas-Valle & Schuurman (2026) count storage space (“opbergruimte”) as part of a good sports place.

  • An agreement with the site ownerPrecedent

    A written adopt-a-space agreement with the municipality or park manager, covering cleaning, lighting hours, toilets and a named contact.

    Why it helps. More hosted use brings more friendly eyes and less damage, and the agreement gives hosts backing without turning them into guards or unpaid staff.

    Sources: Tilburg’s sports park manager pilot (SSNB); Leuven’s steering groups for each urban sport, often led by local role models (ESWAM eMagazine).

  • Light-touch evaluationPrecedent

    Simple counts plus who comes back, how safe people feel and who is missing, through a short anonymous survey and a host log. Optional, and no cameras.

    Why it helps. It shows value beyond headcount and helps find who is not there yet.

    Source: how Eindhoven monitors use of its outdoor sports space with entrance sensors, observation and surveys (Sportinnovator).

From many corners to one room

Comfort is the start, not the goal. What we are after is a floor where newcomers and skilled movers share the same pulse and the whole room starts to move together: one shared groove rather than a stage and an audience.

Some affordances pull in that direction: small zones within earshot of the same sound, so corners can merge as confidence grows; a cypher at the edge rather than in the centre; hosts who invite the watchers in. Others can pull against it. Seating can become a judging gallery, mirrors can make people self-conscious, and too many zones can split the room. That is why we pay attention to what actually happens, and adjust.

What we don’t know yet

  • Many of these are precedents or ideas, not tested results. The labels say which is which.
  • Some evidence comes from other settings: the floor-marking study was with children, and the synchrony studies were controlled experiments, not parks.
  • Every place is different. These work best designed with the people who will use them: local dancers, neighbours, girls and young women, and site owners.
  • None of this is a health promise. It is about good, regular room to move together.
